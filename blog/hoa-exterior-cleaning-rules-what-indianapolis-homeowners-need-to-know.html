HOA Exterior Cleaning Rules: What Indianapolis Homeowners Need to Know

How HOA governing documents typically handle exterior maintenance, and how to stay ahead of a violation notice.

Plenty of Indianapolis-area neighborhoods, especially newer subdivisions in suburbs like Avon, Carmel, and Westfield, are governed by an HOA with specific exterior maintenance expectations. Here's how that typically plays out and how to stay ahead of it.

Where These Rules Actually Live

HOA authority comes from the community's Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs) — the governing document every homeowner agrees to when buying into the community. Exterior maintenance requirements, including expectations around visible algae, mildew, or grime on siding, typically live in this document rather than in any city or state regulation.

What Triggers a Violation Notice

The most common trigger we see is visible black or green streaking on siding — exactly the algae growth covered in our humidity and algae guide. Other common triggers include stained or moss-covered driveways, dirty roof shingles visible from the street, and neglected decks or fences. HOAs generally aren't targeting individual homeowners — they're enforcing a baseline appearance standard across the whole community.

Typical Maintenance Expectations

While every HOA's CC&Rs are different, a common general guideline is cleaning siding and exterior surfaces every 12–18 months to prevent buildup from ever becoming visible enough to trigger a complaint. Communities with strict architectural standards sometimes specify this explicitly; others simply expect a generally well-maintained appearance without a fixed schedule.

What to Do If You Get a Notice

Most HOA violation notices come with a cure period — typically 30 days — to address the issue before further action or fines. Scheduling a cleaning promptly is almost always simpler and cheaper than contesting the notice, especially since algae streaking is rarely disputable once it's visible.

Staying Ahead of It Entirely

The homeowners who never deal with an HOA notice tend to be the ones on a proactive annual cleaning schedule rather than reacting after the fact. If your community has known appearance standards, timing an annual wash for spring (see best time of year to pressure wash) keeps you consistently ahead of any complaint before it happens.

If You're an HOA Board Member

If you're managing enforcement for a whole community rather than a single home, bulk contracting for common areas or coordinating resident discounts is worth exploring — see HOA bulk power washing contracts for how that typically works.
